Object lessons: Understanding and augmenting the role of reproductions in museums

This project will investigate the role of reproductions (digital scans, 3D prints, augmented reality) on the experiences of visitors to Oxford University Museum sites.

InstitutionsGoldsmiths, University of London, Oxford Internet Institute (OII) and Oxford University Museums FundingLeverhulme Trust


Existing theory suggests that original artworks and artefacts held in museums and galleries have a special quality, an ‘aura’ or ‘essence’ arising from their unique history (Benjamin, 1935). Essentialism has previously been shown to undermine the value ascribed to reproductions (Newman & Bloom, 2012). Nevertheless, technologies for reproducing objects in museum collections through digital imaging and 3D printing are fast advancing.

Despite the museum sector’s increasing engagement with reproductions, surprisingly little is known about how visitors experience emerging methods of reproduction and to what extent these experiences are impacted by essentialist attitudes. Existing research and theory therefore suggest a tension between museums’ willingness to embrace technologies for reproduction, and prevailing theory about how visitors might respond to them.

Research objectives

1

Investigate the current and potential uses of reproductions in UK museum and gallery collections

From these insights we will commission new artefact reproductions and tools for use in later experiments. We will also recruit members of an expert panel that will meet regularly throughout the project.

2

Map out the psychological impacts of reproduction type and object category on object experience

To evaluate whether object type (e.g. artistic, functional or natural objects) and reproduction type influence object experiences in the museum and whether this can be explained by essentialist beliefs.

3

Investigate the role of context, interaction, and individual differences of viewers on object experience

Having determined how reproductions are used in cultural collections, and the impact of different kinds of reproductions on object experience, we will explore the role of moderators on the impacts of reproduction, including context, interaction, age, and expertise.

4

Implement the findings into a toolkit for museum professionals

Providing a framework for how to integrate and present reproductions in cultural collections. Guidance will be created in multiple formats including videos, downloadable pdfs and flow-charts of findings.

Methods

Mobile eye-tracking (MET)

Collecting metrics of visual attention while particpants freely move around museum sites.

Interviews

Qualitative interviews with museum and gallery professionals.

Surveys

Encompassing perceptual, cognitive, affective, and communicative experiences of objects.

Museum partnerships

Data collected on-site in partnership with Oxford Univeristy Museums.
